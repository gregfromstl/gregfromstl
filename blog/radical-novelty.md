# AI and Radical Novelty

AI has introduced more uncertainty into the average white collar person's life than ever before. Some have taken it in stride, declaring a new era of AI productivity and rebranding their Twitter profiles to dish out the latest "advice". Others (and it seems like most based on closed door conversations I've had) are struggling to find both productivity and [fulfillment](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50093180) in the work they do. What causes this feeling? The HackerNews thread I linked has a comment that I think sums it up perfectly:
> In times of great change, the set of norms and rules that we take for granted as an organizing principle of our lives get uprooted. It's as if all of the pieces and tokens and rules of the board game we played our whole lives are swept off the table to make room for a new game, but the new game has not been set up yet. In that interim, we all feel a sense of unease. It is our turn to move, but we don't know the rules of the game or what moves to make. We feel intense pressure to play the game well, but don't know how.

I'd summarize this feeling simply as uncertainty. We are facing extreme levels of uncertainty that few generations before us faced. Similar things have happened after the Industrial Revolution and the Internet Revolution -- the comment above is based off Emile Durkheim's book _Suicide_, which was written shortly after the Industrial Revolution. The scale of the current revolution dwarfs those others by an order of magnitude, because these revolutions compound on one another. Post Industrial Revolution the production and distribution of new things is accelerated, so any future revolution now propogates faster. Following the Internet Revolution information is distributed faster, meaning we hear about and how to use new things in seconds rather than days or weeks. So now we sit in the midst of the AI Revolution, being force fed the newest how-to's and harnesses on an hourly basis. This is a wholly unhealthy way of consuming anything new.

### Radical Novelty

Edgar Dijkstra has an excellent talk that calls this sort of change "radical novelty". Radical refers to a change that happens so quickly we can no longer dependably apply analogy or past experience to the novel thing. He uses it as a term to describe computers, and it no doubt applies to AI equally well.

Normally when we encounter a problem we use our past experiences to determine how to proceed. We think of a time when we were in a similar situation and consider what we did then. In communicating about the novelty with others we use analogies to expedite building a mutual intuition and language around the novelty. With the introduction of rapid (radical) change, reality has shifted so much that its no longer accurate to use these past experiences or analogies. The new thing is of completely different origin than anything seen before.

AI applies here on a number of dimensions. While existing software is logical and *programmed* to be as deterministic as possible, models are a gray (nearly black) box *trained* to match a loss function as accurately as possible. One is the implementation of an explicit function while the other is a condensed representation of pre-existing inputs and outputs. Note I'm primarly speaking of next-token supervised prediction models here, and I'm aware different training methods exist. However, all of them are radically novel in their own way.

Is AI like a human then? It has all kinds of "experiences" and uses them to reason about the best course of action? Without going too deep down this rabbit hole I would say absolutely not. Humans have dramatically different reasoning levels and complexity of reasoning than trained models do, no matter what scale the model was trained on.

What are we to do in the face of this? I'm honestly not sure, but I do know two things we *shouldn't* do that nearly everyone is doing:

### Mistake #1: Adopt radical novelty in every way possible as quickly as possible
Silicon Valley loves to preach speed above all. You must ship fast and you must adopt fast. This philosophy isn't the topic of this post so I won't argue one way or another, but I think in the face of radical novelty its a dangerous position to hold. We do not yet have a mental model for where AI fits, and moving critical infrastructure to depend on it is reckless (this includes both your [personal infrastructure]() and [global infrastructure]()). You might say: "How will we ever learn more if we don't try things?" and you would be 100% right. We should try things, we should experiment the hell out of AI. But we should keep it in its place. Its like a new drug, we have to do the necessary lab tests to understand it before widespread use. The best pace for any radical novelty is the pace at which you can fully understand how it works and the implications it has. You won't fall behind if you do this, trust me. No one knows what they're doing and the ones who think they do will have reality slap them upside the head when it arrives.

### Mistake #2: Attempt to reason about radical novelty via analogy or past experience
This is the cardinal sin of radical novelty according to Dijkstra. AI is not like humans, its not like existing programs, it is an entirely new form of intelligence. In an attempt to communicate how "revolutionary" AI is, people have resorted to analogies ad infinitum that have little to no core similarities to AI. Its not a junior employee. Its not a search engine. Its not even a piece of software really. As much as I wish we could put it into a neat little box that perfectly contains its capabilities, we can't and any trying will do more harm than good. Both sides do this, some using negative analogies to showcase its flaws while others use positive ones to hide them. I do think the latter is more dangerous given its momentum. It feels like everyday someone invents a new way to dress AI up to become more human-like. Give it your credit card, make it your personal assistant, add it to your Slack with a cute name. These are all analogies that make it increasingly more difficult to reason about what AI can and should do for us. As difficult as it may be we need to approach any radical novelty from first principles and its component parts. It is something entirely new and any attempt to make it too familiar will hide what it really is.

You might say this whole time in history is symptomatic of humanity's inability to think from first principles or pursue a true understanding of things. The race to appear to know what you're doing has superseded everything else. If we don't start to approach AI as the radical novelty it is we'll build a world where its tragically misunderstood and misused. That world would only bring more uncertainty and unease.
